## 1. Logline

A disgraced Pinkerton detective arrives in a Wyoming railroad town carrying a sealed envelope addressed to a dead man — and finds that the people who sent him there already know what he's going to find.

---

## 2. World

### Wyoming Territory, 1862

The Civil War is happening far to the east and feels abstract here — but it shapes everything. Federal troops are spread thin. Government contracts for the railroad survey are worth a fortune to whoever can secure them. Men with money and connections are operating in a vacuum of accountability.

The Pacific Railroad survey has reached this far west, bringing with it a particular kind of violence: paperwork violence. Forged deeds. False surveys. Land stolen not with guns but with ink and a notary stamp, and then defended with guns when the ink is questioned.

The Shoshone people have lived on this land for generations. They are not absent from this story. They are being erased from it systematically, and the game does not let the player look away from that.

### The railroad as antagonist

The railroad itself is not evil. The men profiting from its advance in ways that were never sanctioned are. This distinction matters — it prevents the story from becoming a simple anti-progress fable and keeps the moral weight where it belongs: on individual choices made by individual people who knew better.

### Blackwood, Wyoming

Population approximately 400. Founded 1858 as a waystation on the Overland Mail route. The railroad survey arrived in 1861 and the town tripled in size in eight months. New money built new buildings on top of old ones. The church has no congregation yet. The jail is always full on Saturday night.

Blackwood is a town that knows it is temporary. Every person in it is either trying to extract something before it collapses or trying to build something that will last. These two groups do not trust each other.

## 3. Tone & influences

### The feeling we are after

The dread that builds before anything happens. The sense that forces larger than any one person are already in motion. The question of whether a decent person can stay decent in an indecent situation — and what it costs them either way.

### Narrative influences

- **Cormac McCarthy** — economy of language, moral weight without moralizing, landscape as active presence
- **Deadwood (HBO)** — period-authentic dialogue cadence, no character purely good or evil, profanity as texture not shock
- **No Country for Old Men** — a protagonist who arrives after something has already been set in motion; the horror of competence without conscience
- **True Grit (2010)** — a revenge plot that keeps undercutting the romance of revenge

### What this is NOT

- Not a redemption arc in the Hollywood sense. Elias does not become a better person because the plot requires it. He may become a clearer one.
- Not a morality meter. The game does not tell the player when they have been good or bad.
- Not revisionist. The history is not softened. The railroad destroyed lives. That is in the text.

## 4. Characters

### Elias Cole — protagonist

**Age:** 34  
**Former role:** Pinkerton National Detective Agency, field agent  
**Discharge:** Involuntary, circumstances undisclosed at game start  
**Physical:** Six feet, lean, scar above left eyebrow. Holds his gun hand near his holster in unfamiliar spaces. Sits with his back to walls.

**Who he is:**

Elias is not a good man trying to be better. He is a precise man trying to figure out what precision is worth when the institutions he served were rotten. He was good at his job. His job turned out to serve people he should not have served. He has not resolved this.

He came west to disappear. He is carrying an envelope that will not let him.

**What the player knows at game start:** Almost nothing. He is on a train. He has an envelope. He used to work for the Pinkertons.

**What the player learns over Act I:** He was discharged after refusing to complete an assignment. The assignment involved Harlan Dross. The refusal cost him everything except his freedom. He does not know yet that he was sent to Blackwood — that this is not a coincidence.

**Voice:** Sparse. Observational. He says half of what he means and means twice what he says. Dry humor that surfaces in unexpected moments. Never self-pitying.

**Player expression:** The player shapes whether Elias approaches the world with suspicion, empathy, or confrontation. His core — precise, not cruel, not dishonest — is fixed. His manner is not.

---

### June Whitehorse — primary ally

**Age:** 28  
**Background:** Half-Shoshone (maternal), half-Scottish (paternal). Grew up in the space between two worlds and fully claimed by neither. Speaks English, Shoshone, and enough Spanish to be useful. Reads better than most men in Blackwood.

**Current role:** Scout and trail guide for the railroad survey — the only work that pays what she needs. She is watching the survey destroy land she knows intimately. She has reasons for this she has not shared.

**Who she is:**

June is the smartest person in most rooms she enters and has learned to let people believe otherwise. She is pragmatic in the way that people who have had to be pragmatic are — not cynical, but clear-eyed. She finds Elias interesting because he is harder to read than most.

She knew Caleb Marsh. She had her own reasons for watching him. When those reasons surface, Elias has to decide whether the relationship can survive them.

**Voice:** Direct. She does not perform warmth she does not feel. When she does show warmth it is specific and therefore credible. She has a precise, almost forensic way of describing what she sees — a professional habit.

**Arc:** June starts the game with her own agenda that runs parallel to Elias's. By Act I's end their agendas have merged. By Act II she is asking a question Elias cannot answer for her: what do you do when justice and survival require different things?

---

### Harlan Dross — Act I antagonist

**Age:** 52  
**Role:** Railroad development agent, Pacific Survey Commission  
**In practice:** Land speculator using the survey as cover

**Who he is:**

Dross is not a villain because he is evil. He is a villain because he is efficient. He has calculated that Caleb Marsh's life was worth less than the land the ledger threatened. He would make the same calculation again without hesitation and without guilt — not because he is a monster but because he genuinely believes that men like Marsh are, in the long run, obstacles to things that matter more than any individual life.

He is charming in formal settings. He remembers names and uses them. He is genuinely knowledgeable about railroad economics and land law. He believes in the railroad the way some men believe in God — as a force larger than human objection.

**Voice:** Measured. He never raises his voice. He asks questions rather than making accusations. He is the most frightening kind of antagonist: one who makes you feel unreasonable for opposing him.

**First appearance:** Chapter 2. He is having dinner alone at the Sawdust & Rye. He acknowledges Elias across the room with a single sentence and leaves. The player should feel surveyed.

**Design rule:** Dross is never seen giving an order to commit violence. His enforcer Terrence exists precisely so Dross's hands stay clean. This is not a coincidence.

---

### Walt Pruitt — sheriff, ambiguous

**Age:** 47  
**Appointed:** Eight months ago, after his predecessor died of "a fever"  
**Family:** Wife and two children in Cheyenne

**Who he is:**

Walt Pruitt is not corrupt in the simple sense. He does not take money from Dross. He takes inaction. He has looked at his situation — a dead predecessor, a powerful man with federal connections, a family he is responsible for — and made a calculation. The calculation is: survive. Do not move against Dross directly. Find ways to be useful to ordinary people that do not threaten the man who can end your employment and possibly your life.

He is not at peace with this. The player can see that he is not at peace with it. That is what makes him interesting.

**Voice:** Careful. He says things that could mean two things and lets the listener decide. He is warmer when no one is watching. He has a habit of looking at the door when he's saying something he knows is true.

**Arc:** Pruitt is the game's most honest portrait of complicity — not the dramatic kind, but the ordinary kind that most people who live in bad systems practice every day. Whether the player helps him find a third option or condemns him for not finding it himself is up to them.

---

### Two-Bit Terrence — Act I boss

**Age:** 38  
**Known name:** Terrence. No known last name.  
**Called "Two-Bit"** because he once killed a man over a two-bit debt and seemed proud of it.  
**Background:** Former Union Army irregular. Served under a commander whose methods were never officially sanctioned.

**Who he is:**

Terrence is not a sadist. He is a professional. He is precise, unhurried, and genuinely dangerous — which is more frightening than cruelty. He does not hate the people he is sent to deal with. He has simply placed them in a category where his personal feelings are irrelevant.

He is intelligent. He plans. He uses the environment. He does not warn people twice.

**Voice:** Sparse even by this game's standards. He says what he needs to say and nothing else. He is not cruel in his language even when he is cruel in his actions.

**Boss design note:** Terrence should be defeatable through combat, stealth, or dialogue. The dialogue path requires the player to have been paying attention. He respects competence — if Elias demonstrates that he has already won, Terrence will hear that argument. See GDD §7.6 for mechanical details.

**Open question:** Is Terrence killable or only incapacitatable? If alive, he becomes one of Act II's most interesting secondary characters — a man who lost cleanly and has to decide what that means. Decision deferred to Milestone 3.

---

### "The Widow" — Act II antagonist (introduced Act I end)

**Age:** Unknown  
**Identity:** Unknown at Act I close  
**Known facts:** She knows who Elias is, why he came to Blackwood, and what he found. She knew before he did.

**Design rule:** The Widow must feel dangerous before the player knows anything about her. The letter at the end of Act I accomplishes this by demonstrating she knows things she should not. She is frightening because she is ahead of everyone.

**Her letter (Act I closing):**

> *"You found what Marsh died for. Now ask yourself who sent you to find it. The answer is the same man who killed him."*

This sentence does three things: it confirms Elias was sent (not going of his own will), it implies Dross was involved in Marsh's death (which the player may or may not have already deduced), and it suggests The Widow knows more about Elias's past than he has told anyone in Blackwood.

### Supporting cast — Act I

**Rev. Solomon Voss**  
Met on the train in Chapter 1. Nervous, sweating despite the cold. Something in his bag he will not discuss. He is going to Blackwood for reasons he describes as "ministry" with a sincerity that is slightly too careful. He knows more than he will say in Chapter 1. Whether the player discovers what is the player's business.

**Pearl Dancy**  
Owner and operator of the Sawdust & Rye. 44, originally from New Orleans. She came to Blackwood three years ago for reasons she describes as "opportunity" and does not elaborate on. She knows everything that happens in Blackwood because her saloon is where everything happens. She is scrupulously neutral in the town's politics and charges the same prices to everyone. She likes Elias because he tips well and does not cause trouble — initially.

**Doc Aldridge**  
Town physician. 58. He signed Caleb Marsh's death certificate. He signed it before he should have. He knows this. He has a half-empty whiskey bottle in his medicine cabinet that was full ten days ago, when Marsh died. He will not speak about it without significant trust established first.

**Cora Marsh**  
Caleb's widow. Lives above the general store. Does not trust strangers. Loved her husband in a way that makes his death a wound she is not ready to examine with anyone she does not know. She has a letter fragment from Caleb that she does not know is a clue.

**Looks-Twice**  
Shoshone elder. Brief appearance in Chapter 3 at the mine trail. Expands significantly in Act II. He has watched Blackwood grow for three years. He has a clear-eyed assessment of what the railroad means that he will share with anyone willing to listen — which is very few people.

## 5. Act summaries

### Act I — Blackwood (MVP)

**The question Act I asks:** Who killed Caleb Marsh, and why?

**The answer Act I gives:** Dross had him killed to protect the ledger of forged land deeds. Terrence did it. The ledger proves systematic fraud that threatens the entire survey commission contract.

**What Act I withholds:** Why Elias was sent. Who sent him. What The Widow knows. What Elias did to get discharged from the Pinkertons.

**Emotional arc:** A stranger arrives. Something is wrong. He investigates because it is in his nature to investigate. By the end he has recovered the ledger, defeated Terrence, and discovered that he is not a free agent in this story — he was placed here.

Full chapter breakdowns in `Docs/Story/Acts/Act1_Blackwood.md`.

---

### Act II — Red Mesa Flats (post-MVP)

**The question Act II asks:** Who is The Widow, and what does she want?

**The central complication:** The ledger can be used in multiple ways. Legal channels — slow, uncertain, and Dross has friends in the survey commission. Blackmail — faster, dirtier, and it keeps Dross alive and dangerous. Give it to June's people — it does not help them legally but it proves, to them, that someone was paying attention.

**Dross confrontation:** The first direct confrontation with Dross. He is not frightened. He makes Elias an offer: the ledger for Elias's Pinkerton record expunged, his discharge reversed. He can do this. The player must decide whether Elias can.

**The Widow reveal (partial):** By Act II's end the player understands The Widow's connection to Elias's Pinkerton discharge. Her full identity and agenda carry into Act III.

Full chapter breakdowns in `Docs/Story/Acts/Act2_RedMesa.md` (to be written).

## 6. Themes

### Trust and complicity

Every person Elias encounters has their own agenda. Helping June means helping her agenda. Helping Pruitt means helping his survival calculus. The game does not offer a position outside this — Elias cannot be purely neutral. Every choice implicates him in something.

### The cost of the railroad

The railroad is not presented as simply good or simply evil. It is presented as a force with consequences that fall unequally. The people who benefit from it are not the people who pay for it. The game asks the player to hold this complexity without resolving it into a bumper sticker.

### What men run from

Elias's backstory is revealed in fragments across both acts. By Act II the player understands why he was discharged — and whether it was deserved is genuinely ambiguous. He did something that was right by one standard and disqualifying by another. The game does not tell the player which standard to use.

### The limits of justice

The ledger is evidence of a crime. Evidence of a crime and accountability for a crime are not the same thing in 1862 Wyoming, where Dross has federal connections and Elias is a disgraced detective. The game's endings differ based on what the player decides justice looks like when the system is compromised.

## 7. The central mystery

### What actually happened to Caleb Marsh

Marsh was a low-level survey commission clerk who began noticing discrepancies in the land deed records Dross was filing. He was not a hero — he was a frightened man who kept a ledger as insurance rather than going to the authorities, because he knew the authorities Dross had access to. He told his wife something was hidden at the mine. He went to retrieve the ledger and Terrence was already there.

### Why Elias was sent

Elias was not sent to deliver an envelope. The envelope was a mechanism to get him to Blackwood. He was sent by someone who knew the ledger existed and needed a person with investigative skills who was outside Pinkerton control — because the Pinkertons, it turns out, have a contractual relationship with the Pacific Survey Commission.

The person who sent him is connected to The Widow. This is the thread that runs from Act I through Act II and into Act III.

### What the torn name means

The name on the envelope was torn off deliberately — not by accident, not by water damage. Someone removed it before giving the envelope to Elias. This was to ensure he would ask questions in Blackwood rather than going directly to the recipient. The questions were the point.

## 8. Dialogue voice guide

### General rules

- **Period authenticity without parody.** Characters speak in 1862 cadences — slightly more formal than modern speech, more given to complete sentences — but they do not sound like a museum exhibit.
- **No character explains themselves.** People in this world do not narrate their motivations. They speak obliquely, with subtext. The player reads between lines.
- **Silence is characterization.** What characters do not say is as important as what they say.

### Character-specific voices

**Elias:** Short sentences. Observational rather than declarative. Dry humor surfaces unexpectedly. Never self-pitying. When he is angry it sounds like calm.

> *"You signed the certificate two days before the examination. I'm not asking why. I'm asking if you knew."*

**June:** Direct. Precise. She describes what she sees, not what she feels. When she does express feeling it arrives without warning and lands hard.

> *"My grandmother's family summered on Red Mesa. Their names aren't in that ledger. Nothing in there says what they lost."*

**Dross:** Measured, warm, asks questions. Never raises his voice. Makes you feel unreasonable for opposing him.

> *"I think you're a man who believes in doing things correctly. I respect that. I'd like to offer you the chance to do this one correctly."*

**Pruitt:** Careful. Says things that could mean two things. Warmer when no one is watching.

> *"Caleb Marsh had debts, son. Best leave that stone unturned."*

**Terrence:** Sparse. Says what he needs to say. Not cruel in language.

> *"You found it. I was hoping you would."*

**Pearl:** Sharp, fair, slightly amused by everything. Does not miss anything.

> *"First night's on me. After that you pay like everyone else. Welcome to Blackwood."*
